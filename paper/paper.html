What Do Lecture-Video Benchmarks Measure? A Controlled Re-implementation and Audit of LectQA-Vid, and Slide Alignment at Parity with MaViLS

Krish Pandit, Rakshit Panwar, Ananya Samant, Vidyansh Gaur

Department of Computer Science and Engineering, The NorthCap University, Gurugram, India

Abstract We built LinkRAG, a retrieval-augmented question-answering system for lecture material that links speech, slides and documents at indexing time, and evaluated it against two published systems on their own benchmarks. On LectQA-Vid, the benchmark of Intra-Video Temporal-Aware RAG, we re-implemented the published pipeline and compared every method with one answering model. Hybrid retrieval with one iterative re-query scores +2.00 token-F1 (95 % CI [+0.96, +3.11]) above the re-implemented pipeline; +1.74 of that comes from the re-query. We could not reproduce the benefit reported for timestamp-constrained retrieval: where the temporal filter keeps evidence it makes no measurable difference, and it leaves 21 % of the questions it applies to with no context. An audit shows that the benchmark cannot measure retrieval at all. Giving the model the whole transcript beats every retrieval method, echoing the question as the answer scores 22.87 F1 against a published 23.52, and the correct multiple-choice option is the first option in 99.7 % of questions and the longest in 94.6 %. On MaViLS, a video-to-slide alignment benchmark, a fusion of image, frame-OCR and speech features reaches a mean F1 of 0.853 against the published 0.80 on the same nine held-out lectures; the paired difference (+0.055, CI [−0.050, +0.161]) is parity, and the video frames, not the speech or the decoder, carry it. The linking and set-level reranking components of our own system show no measured benefit on either benchmark, and we report that as a negative result. We release the code, the stored answers and the scripts that reproduce every number.

1. Introduction

Lecture recordings are a natural target for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [1]: the content is long, it is spread over speech, slides and written notes, and a student's question is usually answered by a specific moment or slide. Two recent lines of work address parts of this problem. Intra-Video Temporal-Aware RAG [2] answers questions about a single lecture video by restricting retrieval to a temporal window, and introduces the LectQA-Vid benchmark. MaViLS [3] aligns each spoken sentence of a lecture with the slide on screen, and provides a hand-labelled benchmark for that task.

We set out to improve on both with LinkRAG, a system whose design centres on an evidence linking layer: typed, scored links between speech segments, slides and figures are built at indexing time, retrieval follows them, and a reranker selects an evidence set for coverage rather than scoring each unit alone. To make any gain attributable, we measured the system only on the two target papers' own benchmarks, with their metrics.

The outcome is not the one we designed for, and we report it as it is. Our contributions are:

  1. A controlled re-implementation of Temporal-Aware RAG on LectQA-Vid with one answering model for every method, and an attribution of the observed gap to its parts (§5.2–5.3).
  2. An audit of LectQA-Vid showing that neither its multiple-choice nor its open-ended half can measure the value of retrieval, with three simple baselines that any future use of the benchmark should report (§5.1, §5.4).
  3. A multi-feature slide alignment at parity with MaViLS, with a decomposition showing which features and which decoder produce the result (§6).
  4. A negative result for link-following retrieval and set-level reranking on single-stream benchmarks, and a score-scale defect in complementarity reranking that inflated an earlier estimate of its cost (§7).

2. Related Work

RAG over video and lectures. Temporal-Aware RAG [2] segments a transcript and frame captions into timestamped chunks, retrieves the top-K by embedding similarity, filters them to a temporal window (their eq. 22), re-ranks with a cross-encoder and answers with a language model. It reports that the temporal filter is its most important component. MI-RAG [4] alternates retrieval and reasoning, issuing follow-up queries from intermediate answers; we use one round of such re-querying as a component. MARA [5] studies adaptive retrieval for multi-page document question answering. Knowledge-graph approaches to educational material [6, 7] extract entities and relations with a language model; our links are structural (co-occurrence in time, layout, deixis) and need no extraction model. MultiHaystack [8] benchmarks retrieval over a large mixed corpus of images, videos and documents.

Speech-to-slide alignment. MaViLS [3] computes three sentence-by-slide similarity matrices, from OCR text, image features and the audio transcript, and decodes a slide sequence with dynamic programming under a slide-jump penalty. The Lecture Presentations Multimodal dataset [9] poses cross-modal retrieval between spoken language and slide figures, and narrated-lecture generation [10] aligns narration with slide regions.

Building blocks. We use BM25 [11] and BGE-M3 dense embeddings [12] fused by reciprocal rank fusion [13], Whisper speech recognition [14], Sentence-BERT models [15] for the similarity metric that [2] defines, SwiftFormer [16] image features as in [3], and maximal marginal relevance [17] as a reranking ablation.

3. System

Ingestion and indexing. Documents, slide decks, images and audio become evidence units with a modality and a location (page and box, or start and end time). Speech is transcribed locally with word timestamps and packed into sentence-aligned segments. Units are embedded with BGE-M3 and indexed for exact dense search and BM25.

Evidence linking layer. Four link types are built at indexing time: audio–slide, from a monotone dynamic-programming alignment of speech segments to slides over a hybrid similarity; figure–text, between a figure and the paragraph that explains it; deictic, between a spoken pointing phrase and the visual element on screen; and same-slide. A relatedness gate tests each recording–deck pair against a shuffled null before its links are admitted.

Retrieval and reranking. The baseline is reciprocal rank fusion of dense and BM25 rankings. Link-following retrieval expands the top seeds by one hop along the links. A complementarity reranker then selects a set of k units greedily, scoring relevance plus a bonus for each newly covered modality and each link into the set, minus within-modality redundancy. Iterative retrieval issues one follow-up query written by the language model [4].

Answering and verification. The answerer returns an answer as a list of claims, each citing evidence units. A separate judge model checks every claim against the text it cites and must quote a supporting span.

Every stage runs in a baseline and a linked mode from the same code, so that each component is an ablation.

4. Experimental Setup

LectQA-Vid. The benchmark [2] has 100 computer-science lecture videos of 2–5 minutes and 3,000 question–answer pairs, half multiple choice and half open-ended, at three difficulty levels. 95 videos were still available, with 2,832 pairs. The published split and 1,000-pair evaluation subset are not released, so open-ended answers are evaluated on a stratified sample of 300 questions (100 per level, fixed seed) from 93 videos, three repeats each; the 1,414 multiple-choice questions are run once with options shuffled under a fixed seed. We use the metric suite of [2]: token F1, BLEU-4 [18], METEOR [19], ROUGE-1 [20] and MiniLM cosine similarity.

Re-implementation of [2]. The code of [2] is not public. We rebuilt its pipeline from the paper with the values its Table 3 gives: semantic chunking, BGE-base embeddings, top-10 candidates above cosine 0.6, the temporal filter with a 3 s tolerance, merging of adjacent results, cross-encoder re-ranking and a temporally ordered context. Where the paper is silent the choices are ours: the re-rank pool and context sizes, the merge gap, the cross-encoder, and OCR in place of generated frame captions. Two points matter for the results. The paper does not say where the filter's query interval comes from at inference; we give it the question's annotated interval, the only interval the paper describes. It also does not say what happens when no chunk survives; our re-implementation then returns a fixed "no evidence" reply. Every mode is answered by the same model (gpt-5.4-mini, temperature 0) with the same prompt. The answering model of [2] is not named, so absolute scores are not comparable with its tables; differences between our rows are.

MaViLS. The benchmark [3] has 20 lectures with every spoken sentence labelled with a slide. We use the authors' scorer unchanged. The lectures were split once into 10 for tuning and 10 for testing; all weights and thresholds were set on the tuning half. The release contains no video for one test lecture, so test means cover nine lectures and are paired with the per-lecture figures published in [3].

Statistics. We report 95 % bootstrap intervals [21] from 10,000 resamples. Paired differences resample the sampled unit, whole videos or whole lectures, because questions on one video share a transcript. An interval that contains zero is reported as no measurable difference.

Only two steps are measurable. The re-query, a technique from [4], accounts for most of our advantage; without it the two retrieval pipelines are level. The other measurable step is not a property of retrieval quality: it is the cost of answering "no evidence" when the temporal filter removes every candidate.

The temporal filter. In [2], removing the filter lowers F1 from 23.52 to 11.40, and a baseline without timestamps scores 14.80. We do not reproduce this. The filter applied to 160 of our 300 questions and left 34 of them (21 %) with no context. In all 34 a chunk inside the window existed and was removed earlier, by the similarity threshold (18) or the top-10 cut (16). Where the filter kept something, its effect is −0.53 [−1.30, +0.20]. On the 34 empty-window questions our own retrieval overlaps the annotated interval for 32 %, against 87 % on the other 126, and a full-transcript answer cites a unit inside the interval for 28 % against 77 %. A check of all 34, together with 16 other passing stamps as a control, located each reference answer in the timestamped transcript (by a language model, without showing it the stamp) and compared the location with the stamp: for 27 of the 34 the stamp misses the spoken answer or the answer is never said, against 4 of the 16 controls. Most empty windows are therefore annotation problems, not retrieval failures. Our reading is that a filter driven by an annotated interval is only as good as the annotation, and that the benefit reported in [2] depends on details of the interval source and the empty-result behaviour that the paper does not specify.

5.4 The benchmark cannot measure retrieval

Three observations bound what an open-ended score on LectQA-Vid means. First, the full transcript beats our best retrieval by 2.04 F1 [+1.08, +3.06] at every difficulty level. Transcripts run from about 140 to 1,544 tokens (median 780), and all 95 together are 72,926 tokens, so any current model can read a whole video, or the whole corpus, at once; retrieval can only remove information. Second, answering each question with its own text scores 22.87 F1, because reference answers restate the question. The usable range is therefore about 23 to 39 F1, and the published overall score of 23.52 lies at its floor. Third, only 1,598 of the 2,832 annotated intervals pass basic format and range checks, which limits any timestamp-based evaluation.

A closed-book control points the same way. We gave a second model, Claude Haiku 5.5, the same 300 questions under a prompt that allows its own knowledge, once with no context and once with the full transcript (one run, so direction only; the model answered in batches rather than one call per question). With no context it scores 28.07 F1, 75 % of the 37.55 it reaches with the transcript. The video adds 9.48 points [+7.91, +11.06] overall, but only 4.03 [+1.84, +6.16] on the very hard questions.

We recommend that results on LectQA-Vid be accompanied by a full-transcript row, a question-echo row, a closed-book row and, for multiple choice, a longest-option row.

5.5 Localisation and a pooled-corpus stress test

Without an answerer, we scored whether a retrieved unit overlaps the annotated interval on the 1,598 questions with a checkable interval. Plain hybrid top-k reaches 45.6 % hit@1 and 85.1 % hit@8. Link-following with the complementarity reranker lowers hit@3 from 68.7 % to 63.7 % (−5.01 points [−7.60, −2.24]) and makes no measurable difference at hit@8. Recovering a slide deck from the video frames and linking it to the speech did not help: the alignment picks the slide on screen for only 22.5 % of speech segments, because spoken sentences and OCR text of animated frames share little vocabulary.

As an exploratory stress test we pooled all 95 videos into one index and asked each question without its video. The interval hit rate at 8 falls from 85.1 % to 52.8 %, so this setting does separate retrieval methods. Plain top-k remains the best method: the full linked system is 6.88 points lower [−10.12, −4.03], and the links alone make no measurable difference (−0.38 [−1.13, +0.54]). Most questions refer to "the video" or "the speaker" (2,102 of 2,832), so this setting is not a substitute for a multi-source benchmark.

For each sentence we take the video frame at its timestamp and score it against every slide by SwiftFormer image similarity and by BM25 between the frame's OCR text and the slide's; a third matrix scores the spoken sentence against slide text. The three are fused with weights chosen on the tuning half (0.25, 0.50, 0.25) and decoded by dynamic programming. A visibility gate neutralises the two frame channels when a frame shows no slide.

The fused system reaches 0.853 against 0.80 for the all-features result of [3] on the same nine lectures: 5 wins, 1 tie and 3 losses, a paired difference of +0.055 with CI [−0.050, +0.161]. The interval contains zero, so we report parity. An exploratory leave-one-lecture-out analysis over all 19 lectures with video gives +0.050 [+0.002, +0.103], with 9 wins, 2 ties and 8 losses; three lectures on which [3] scores between 0.53 and 0.65 carry the difference, and without them it is +0.006. The largest per-lecture gains are on Phonetics (0.99 against 0.65) and Solar resource (0.81 against 0.53); the largest loss is on Short range (0.58 against 0.80).

What produces the result. The frames do. Frame OCR alone reaches 0.818, and removing the speech channel costs 0.011 [−0.034, +0.005], which is not measurable. This agrees with the feature ranking in [3], where OCR is the strongest single feature and audio the weakest. The decoder is not a differentiator: on our fused matrix the decoder of [3], run from the authors' code, and ours differ by −0.004 [−0.015, +0.007] over 19 lectures, and on the authors' own text similarity matrix the two score 0.513 and 0.520. A jump-penalised decoder of either kind adds about 0.06 over no decoder. Unlike LectQA-Vid, the benchmark has no trivial floor: mapping sentence position linearly onto the deck scores 0.21.

Failure analysis. On the tuning half, the four weakest lectures are camera-heavy. Between 47 % and 82 % of their errors fall on frames that show no slide, which make up 25 % to 52 % of their frames, and 73 % to 100 % of errors are within two slides of the correct one. Speaker shots, during which only the speech channel and the decoder decide, are the open problem for this task.

7. Discussion

Linking and set-level reranking. Neither component of our design improved retrieval on either benchmark. On LectQA-Vid a question concerns one video with no separate deck or document, so cross-file links do not exist, and the temporal links available there add nothing that hybrid retrieval does not already find. MaViLS evaluates alignment, not question answering. The case the linking layer was designed for, evidence spread over several files that does not fit in the model's context, is not represented in either benchmark, so our result is that the components do not help on single-stream lecture video, not that they cannot help.

A score-scale pitfall. The complementarity reranker adds fixed bonuses (0.3 per new modality) and penalties (0.4 times redundancy) to relevance scores. Reciprocal rank fusion yields scores near 0.03, so on raw scores the bonuses decide the selection almost alone. An early measurement made this way showed a 13-point loss of hit@3; on rank-normalised scores the loss is about 5 points. Set-level objectives that mix relevance with structural terms should normalise relevance first, and ablations should check that all compared conditions see scores on one scale.

What the comparison with [2] supports. Our result is a comparison between two pipelines under one answerer, not an improvement over the numbers in [2], which we could not reproduce in absolute terms. The gain it shows belongs mostly to iterative re-querying.

8. Limitations

  • The pipeline of [2] is our re-implementation; several of its parameters and the source of its filter interval are our choices. With the authors' code or their evaluation subset the comparison could differ.
  • Open-ended results cover 300 of the 1,418 open-ended questions, with one answering model; the closed-book control uses a different model, in a single run.
  • The timestamp check was done by a language model locating answers in the transcript, not by a person, and covers 75 intervals.
  • MaViLS test results rest on nine lectures, compared with published per-lecture figures rather than a re-run, and the test half was examined in several rounds, although no parameter was set on it.
  • Faithfulness was judged once per claim by a single model.

9. Conclusion

Measured on its two target benchmarks, a link-centred lecture RAG system matches a published slide aligner and exceeds a re-implemented video-QA pipeline by two F1 points, and neither result is due to linking. The more durable findings concern the benchmarks. LectQA-Vid can be answered from the whole transcript, from option length, and nearly as well as its published system by repeating the question, so it cannot rank retrieval methods; MaViLS is sound but rewards frame-to-slide matching and leaves speaker shots unsolved. A benchmark that could test evidence linking needs questions whose evidence spans several sources and exceeds one context window. Building one is the next step.
