LinkRAG: Cross-Modal Evidence Linking for Question Answering over Lecture Recordings, Slides and Notes

Rakshit Panwar, Krish Pandit, Ananya Samant, Vidyansh Gaur

Department of Computer Science and Engineering, The NorthCap University, Gurugram, India

Abstract The answer to a question about a lecture is often split across the recording, the slide deck and the accompanying notes, yet retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) scores each piece of evidence on its own, and existing lecture question-answering benchmarks contain no question that needs more than one file. This paper proposes LinkRAG, a lecture RAG system built around an Evidence Linking Layer. Typed, scored links between speech segments, slides, figures and document paragraphs are built at indexing time, and links between a recording and a deck are admitted only when the pair passes a shuffled-null relatedness test. Retrieval follows these links, and a complementarity reranker selects an evidence set for modality coverage and linked support instead of ranking units independently. To test the proposal in the setting it targets, the paper introduces XFile, to the authors' knowledge the first lecture benchmark whose questions are verified to need more than one file: 86 questions over seven topics, each pairing a lecture recording and its slides with a separate textbook or paper chapter, 53 of them cross-file; the held-out test is 38 questions (21 cross-file) on three topics. On LectQA-Vid, LinkRAG's retrieval with one iterative re-query scores 2.00 token-F1 above a controlled re-implementation of Temporal-Aware RAG under the same answering model. On MaViLS, the alignment behind its audio–slide links, extended with video-frame channels, matches the published system. On XFile's three held-out topics, linking changed what was retrieved, adding about one modality to each evidence set, but not the answers: LinkRAG answered all 38 test questions exactly as plain retrieval did, and scored 9.5 points below the pre-registered no-links control on cross-file questions; three topics are too few to establish either difference. An audit of LectQA-Vid shows why single-video benchmarks cannot test linking: the whole transcript beats every retrieval method, and simply repeating the question scores about as well as the published system. The code, the benchmark and the stored model replies are released.

Keywords: retrieval-augmented generation; lecture question answering; multimodal retrieval; evidence linking; cross-file benchmark; speech-to-slide alignment

1. Introduction

1.1 Problem statement

A university course leaves its knowledge in several files: the recording of each lecture, the slide deck shown during it, and the textbook chapter or paper that the lecture follows. A student's question rarely respects these boundaries. In a psychology lecture on research methods, the speaker shows a graph and says "you can see" how the risk of schizophrenia rises with the father's age, giving the odds aloud: 1 in 141 for fathers under 25, 1 in 50 for fathers in their 50s. The study the graph comes from, and the age by which onset is counted, are printed only on the slide's figure. This XFile question, drawn in Figure 1, serves as a running illustration of the design intent; other questions also need the textbook chapter that the lecture follows. A correct answer then needs a sentence of speech, a region of a slide and often a paragraph of a document, and it needs them together.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [1] answers questions from retrieved evidence, but standard retrievers score every unit independently against the question. A spoken sentence that says "you can see" names neither the study nor the graph, and the figure it points at shares few words with the speech, so the pieces that belong together are retrieved separately or not at all. The top-ranked set is often several paraphrases from one source. Published lecture systems work within one stream: Temporal-Aware RAG [2] retrieves from a single video, and MaViLS [3] aligns one recording with one deck. The benchmarks that accompany them contain no question whose evidence spans several files, so whether connecting the files helps has not been measured.

This paper addresses two problems: how to connect evidence across the files of a lecture so that retrieval can assemble a cross-file evidence set, and how to measure whether doing so improves answers.

1.2 Significance of the study

Recorded lectures are a common study resource, and they are slow to search: the answer may be one sentence in an hour of speech, a slide shown for half a minute, or a paragraph in a handout. A system that answers with the moment, the slide and the page it relied on lets a student check the answer instead of trusting it. Whether such systems work is decided on benchmarks. A benchmark that cannot distinguish good retrieval from none misdirects the field, because methods get tuned to scores that the retrieval step does not move.

1.3 Proposed approach and objectives

The proposed system, LinkRAG, builds an explicit graph of evidence links at indexing time. Speech segments are aligned to the slides shown while they are spoken, spoken pointing phrases are linked to the visual element on screen, figures are linked to the paragraphs that explain them, and a statistical gate decides whether a recording and a deck belong together before any link between them is admitted. At question time, retrieval follows these links from the best-matching units, and a set-level reranker chooses the final evidence for coverage and linked support. The objectives were:

  1. to design an Evidence Linking Layer that connects speech, slides, figures and documents with typed, scored links, and that refuses links between unrelated files (§3.2.2);
  2. to design a reranker that selects an evidence set for modality coverage, linked support and low redundancy (§3.2.4);
  3. to construct a benchmark whose questions are verified to need evidence from more than one file (§3.1.1);
  4. to evaluate the system on that benchmark against a no-links control, and on two published benchmarks against the published systems (§4);
  5. to establish what the published benchmarks can and cannot measure (§4.2.3, §5.2).

1.4 Research contributions

  1. An Evidence Linking Layer with four link types (audio–slide, figure–text, deictic, same-slide) and a shuffled-null relatedness gate for each pair of files, built without any extraction model (§3.2.2).
  2. A complementarity set reranker that scores an evidence set rather than single units, and a requirement for such rerankers found while evaluating it: relevance must be normalised before structural terms are added, or a no-links control becomes unstable (§3.2.4, §5.3).
  3. XFile, a cross-file lecture question-answering benchmark: seven topics, each with a recording, its slides and a separate notes chapter; 86 questions verified by per-source answering, 53 of them cross-file; human review and audit; a fixed tune/test split (§3.1.1, §4.1).
  4. An evaluation on the two published benchmarks: the alignment behind the audio–slide links, extended with video-frame channels, reaches parity with MaViLS, and its text-only form, used for the links, matches the published audio-only result; a controlled re-implementation of Temporal-Aware RAG attributes the measured difference to its parts, and an audit shows that LectQA-Vid cannot rank retrieval methods (§4.2, §4.3).

Main results. LinkRAG's retrieval outperforms a controlled re-implementation of Temporal-Aware RAG on LectQA-Vid by 2.00 token-F1, and its audio–slide alignment, extended with video-frame channels, is on par with MaViLS. On XFile, links change what is retrieved, adding modalities to each evidence set, but on the held-out topics they did not improve answers, and three topics are too few to establish a difference in either direction (§4.1, §5.1).

Section 4 reports results on XFile, LectQA-Vid and MaViLS. Section 5 discusses them, Section 6 states the limitations and Section 7 concludes.

2. Related Work and Research Gap

2.1 RAG over video and lectures

Temporal-Aware RAG [2] segments a transcript and frame captions into timestamped chunks, retrieves the top-K by embedding similarity, filters them to a temporal window (their eq. 22), re-ranks with a cross-encoder and answers with a language model. It reports that the temporal filter is its most important component, and it introduces the LectQA-Vid benchmark. MI-RAG [4] alternates retrieval and reasoning, issuing follow-up queries from intermediate answers; one round of such re-querying is used here as an optional component.

2.2 Multi-source and structured retrieval

MARA [5] studies adaptive retrieval for multi-page document question answering. Knowledge-graph approaches to educational material [6, 7] extract entities and relations with a language model before retrieval. The links proposed here are structural instead: they come from co-occurrence in time, page layout and spoken pointing, and need no extraction model. MultiHaystack [8] benchmarks retrieval over a large mixed corpus of images, videos and documents, but its questions are not built around lectures or around evidence that spans files.

2.3 Speech-to-slide alignment

MaViLS [3] computes three sentence-by-slide similarity matrices, from OCR text, image features and the audio transcript, and decodes a slide sequence with dynamic programming under a slide-jump penalty. The Lecture Presentations Multimodal dataset [9] poses cross-modal retrieval between spoken language and slide figures, and narrated-lecture generation [10] aligns narration with slide regions. Alignment is the first step of the proposed linking layer, which turns its output into retrievable links.

2.4 Building blocks

The system uses BM25 [11] and BGE-M3 dense embeddings [12] fused by reciprocal rank fusion [13], Whisper speech recognition [14], Sentence-BERT models [15] for the similarity metric defined in [2], SwiftFormer [16] image features as in [3], and maximal marginal relevance [17] as a reranking ablation.

2.5 Research gap

Three gaps follow from this review (Table 1). First, no lecture system connects a recording to the slides and documents that accompany it and follows those connections at retrieval time; the systems that connect educational sources need a language model to extract a graph first. Second, no lecture benchmark contains questions verified to need more than one file, so a cross-file method cannot be tested. Third, the published lecture benchmark reports no controls that would show whether retrieval matters, and its code, evaluation subset and answering model are not public. This work addresses the first gap with the Evidence Linking Layer, the second with XFile, and the third with a controlled re-implementation and an audit.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1 Materials and datasets

Three datasets are used. XFile is constructed in this work to test cross-file retrieval; LectQA-Vid and MaViLS are the benchmarks of the two target papers.

3.1.1 XFile: a cross-file lecture benchmark

Corpus. XFile pairs lectures from the MaViLS release [3], each with its transcript and slide deck, with a separately authored notes chapter on the same subject (Table 2). The notes are openly available textbook chapters or papers, cut to the pages that cover the lecture, and each download is pinned by its SHA-256 hash. Eight topics were chosen for diagram-heavy decks and a matching notes source. One topic, on medical image processing, was dropped before any question was written because its deck is image-only and yields no slide text. The remaining seven topics give 21 files and 2,210 evidence units: 1,218 speech segments, 756 text blocks and 236 figures. All seven are ingested into one pooled index, so retrieval must find the right lecture as well as the right unit.

Intake gate. A cross-file benchmark is only meaningful if each file adds something the others do not. A topic whose speaker reads the slides aloud gives no reason to retrieve the slides. Each topic was therefore tested before its questions were used: 50 deck sentences were sampled, and a judge model decided, in three runs with a majority vote, whether the transcript states each one. A topic is kept when the upper end of the 95 % Wilson interval is below 65 % and rejected when the lower end is above it; otherwise every deck sentence is judged. Six topics were kept (Table 2). Two topics were borderline on the sample and were decided on the full count: psychology research methods was kept and the Lightning lecture rejected. Lightning's questions never enter a reported number.

Question construction. Figure 2 shows the four stages, numbered as in the figure.

① Drafting. For each topic, a language model (Claude Opus) drafted 14 questions with reference answers that state only the facts asked for, together with the evidence locations: about eight questions that combine a fact from one file with a fact from another, two deictic questions whose referent exists only on a slide the speaker points at, and four single-source controls, one or more per file. The drafter checked by text search that each fact it attributed to one file did not occur in the others. ② Human review. A model pre-screen recommended accept, edit or reject for each of the 98 drafts; the reviewers, two members of the project team, first adopted these recommendations and then read every draft individually against its slides, transcript and notes, which changed no decision. All eight rejected drafts joined two unrelated facts into one question, and one answer was edited, leaving 90. ③ Gold check. Every question was then answered by a second model from each single source in full (the whole transcript, the whole deck with figure OCR, or the whole notes chapter) and from all three together, and a third model graded each answer against the reference, with three runs and a majority vote. A cross-file label survives only if every single-source answer fails and the all-source answer passes. A question that one source alone answers is relabelled as a single-source question of that source, and a question that even all sources cannot answer is dropped. The same check confirms each evidence unit by requiring the judge to quote a supporting span. Four questions were dropped and six relabelled, leaving 86. ④ Verified cross-file subset. 53 questions need more than one file: 44 combine facts across files and 9 are deictic. The other 33 are single-source controls, spread over the three files, which test that linking does no harm when one file suffices. A human audit samples 10 % of the per-unit verdicts, at least five per question type and five from each of six topics, and each reviewer audits the other's topics. All 50 sampled verdicts agree with the judge (50/50); one auditor note records that figure OCR missed small axis labels that the slide image shows.

Split. Before any answer was scored, the topics were split at random (fixed seed) into a tune half and a test half; the draw put Lightning in the test half, and the intake gate later removed it (Fig. 2b). Any parameter may be set only on tune topics and is applied once to test topics. For this evaluation all parameters were frozen at their defaults before any tune result existed, so no tuning took place. One earlier exploratory comparison measured retrieval only, on all seven topics, with the questions not yet reviewed and no answers scored; no parameter was set from it. The test half is gp_scaling, mimic_data and rl_mdp, with 21 cross-file and 17 single-source questions.

3.1.2 LectQA-Vid

The benchmark of [2] has 100 computer-science lecture videos of 2–5 minutes and 3,000 question–answer pairs, about half multiple choice (1,489 in the released file) and half open-ended, at three difficulty levels. It distributes question–answer pairs and YouTube links. Each video was fetched as an audio stream and a video-only stream of at most 360p, and 95 of the 100 links were still available, with 2,832 pairs. The published split and 1,000-pair evaluation subset are not released, so open-ended answers are evaluated on a stratified sample of 300 questions (100 per level, fixed seed) from 93 videos, with three repeats each. The 1,414 multiple-choice questions are run once, with options shuffled under a fixed seed.

3.1.3 MaViLS

The benchmark of [3] has 20 lectures, with every spoken sentence labelled with the slide on screen. Its public repository provides the decks and the labelled transcript sentences, and its archive has video for 19 of the 20 lectures. The lectures were split once into 10 for tuning and 10 for testing, and all weights and thresholds were set on the tune half. One test lecture has no video, so test means cover nine lectures and are paired with the per-lecture figures published in [3].

3.1.4 Preprocessing

Speech. LectQA-Vid audio is transcribed on the CPU with Whisper small [14] (faster-whisper, int8) with word timestamps. For XFile and MaViLS the released sentence transcripts are used, with word times spread evenly within each sentence. Words are packed into sentence-aligned segments of at most 15 s, and each transcript is computed once and frozen, so every method reads the same text.

Frames. For LectQA-Vid a frame is sampled every 5 s and kept only if it differs visibly from the last kept frame. Kept frames are read with Tesseract OCR and become units that last until the next kept frame; [2] used generated frame captions instead. For MaViLS the frame at each sentence's midpoint is used, as in the authors' code.

Documents and slides. PDFs are read with PyMuPDF as text blocks with their page and bounding box, and figures are extracted as images and read with OCR. A file is treated as a slide deck when its pages are mostly landscape, or when the corpus configuration says so. Every unit records its source file and location, and its identifier is the citation the answerer uses.

3.2 Proposed methodology

Figure 3 shows the proposed pipeline. It has two phases, and the steps below follow the numbers in the figure.

At indexing time, ① the sources of a course (recordings, slide decks and notes) are ② converted into evidence units, each with a modality and an exact location. ③ The units are indexed for dense and sparse search, and ④ the Evidence Linking Layer connects units across modalities and files. At question time, ⑤ hybrid retrieval finds seed units, ⑥ link expansion adds the units linked to those seeds, ⑦ the set reranker chooses the evidence the answerer will see, ⑧ the answerer writes an answer as claims that cite evidence units, and ⑨ a verifier checks every claim against the units it cites. Optionally, the answerer may write one follow-up query, which re-enters retrieval at ⑤. The proposed components are ④, ⑥ and ⑦. Every stage runs in a baseline and a linked mode from the same code, so each component can be removed on its own as an ablation.

3.2.1 Evidence units and the index (steps ①–③)

A unit is the smallest citable piece of evidence: a speech segment with its start and end time, a text block with its page and box, or a figure with its page and box and its OCR text. Units are embedded with BGE-M3 [12] for exact inner-product search, and indexed for BM25 [11] over the same text.

3.2.2 Evidence Linking Layer (step ④)

The layer builds four types of directed, scored links. Figure 4 shows one link of each type, taken from the graph built on XFile.

① Audio–slide. Each speech segment of a recording is aligned to a slide of a deck by dynamic programming over a segment-by-slide similarity matrix,

S(i, j) = 0.6 · cos(ei, ej) + 0.25 · BM25norm(i, j) + 0.15 · IDF-overlap(i, j),(1)

where e are BGE-M3 embeddings. The decoder maximises the summed similarity along a path that mostly moves forward through the deck, with small penalties for skipping slides, leaving a slide unused and moving back (at most two slides). Each segment receives a link to its aligned slide, scored by S (Fig. 4, ①).

Relatedness gate. In a pooled corpus, the alignment runs for every recording against every deck, and a dynamic-programming path always exists, even between unrelated files. Before any audio–slide link is admitted, the mean similarity along the path is compared with the same quantity for 30 alignments against randomly shuffled slide orders π. With Φ(a, d) the mean similarity along the decoded path of recording a against deck d, the pair is admitted only if

z(a, d) = (Φ(a, d) − μπ) / σπ ≥ τ,(2)

where μπ and σπ are the mean and standard deviation of Φ over the shuffled orders. The threshold τ = 1.27 was set on MaViLS, one lecture pair at a time, as the smallest value that admits at most 5 % of 380 unrelated recording–deck pairs. Those MaViLS lectures include the seven XFile lectures, so the gate was not calibrated independently of XFile's files, and it still made six errors once the seven were pooled. Figure 5 shows the gate on XFile.

Read from left to right, Figure 5 places each of the 49 recording–deck pairs at its z score. The upper row holds the seven pairs of a recording with its own deck; the lower row holds the 42 pairs of different lectures. Pairs to the right of the dashed line are admitted. Five recordings are correctly linked to their own decks, and 38 of the 42 unrelated pairs are correctly rejected. The gate also makes six errors: it rejects the own decks of mimic_data and visual_system, and admits four pairs of different lectures. Section 5.4 discusses these errors.

② Same-slide. Every figure on a slide is linked to that slide's text with score 1.0, because co-location on a slide is certain rather than estimated.

③ Figure–text. A figure is linked to a paragraph that explains it, scored as

F = 0.30 · ref + 0.15 · layout + 0.15 · page + 0.25 · cos + 0.15 · overlap,(3)

where ref detects an explicit reference such as "Figure 3" on the same or an adjacent page, layout rewards a small vertical gap on the same page, page decays as exp(−|Δp|/2) with page distance, cos is the embedding similarity and overlap the IDF-weighted keyword overlap with the figure's OCR text. Links with F ≥ 0.50 are kept. Across documents, the semantic terms are gated in the same way as audio–slide links, against word-shuffled text (Fig. 4, ③).

④ Deictic. Spoken pointing phrases ("this arrow here", "the box on the right", "as you can see") are detected with a generated grammar of determiner × object noun × direction and three cue tiers. Each detected phrase is linked to a visual element of the slide aligned with that moment,

D = 0.45 · on-slide + 0.15 · cue + 0.20 · cos + 0.20 · overlap,(4)

where on-slide is 1 when the element is on the aligned slide, cue is the tier weight (1.0, 0.6 or 0.3), and the last two terms compare the speech with the element's OCR text. Links with D ≥ 0.45 are kept. Each unit keeps at most eight links. On XFile the layer built 2,222 links: 1,540 audio–slide, 527 figure–text, 140 same-slide and 15 deictic.

3.2.3 Hybrid retrieval and link expansion (steps ⑤–⑥)

The baseline retriever fuses the dense and BM25 rankings by reciprocal rank fusion [13],

rel(u) = Σr ∈ {dense, BM25} 1 / (κ + rankr(u)),    r̂el(u) = (n − ρ(u) + 1) / n,(5)

with κ = 60 and ranks counted from 1, and retrieves a pool of n = 20 candidates; r̂el is the rank-normalised relevance used for link expansion, with ρ(u) the fused rank. Without reranking, the eight best are passed to the answerer, and a reranker instead selects its eight from the whole pool. Link expansion adds to the pool every unit linked to a seed by one hop. A neighbour reached from seed s by a link of score w is scored as

score(v) = rel(s) · w · δ,(6)

with decay δ = 0.5 and rel(s) taken as r̂el(s) from Eq. 5, because raw fusion scores are about 0.03 while link scores are 0.5–1.0. Expansion is additive: it never removes a seed. With these settings no neighbour can enter the top eight on score alone: by Eq. 6 with w ≤ 1 a neighbour scores at most half its seed's relevance, while the eighth seed of a 20-candidate pool has rank-normalised relevance 0.65. Without reranking, the linked retriever therefore returns exactly the baseline's units by construction, and links act only through the reranker.

3.2.4 Complementarity set reranking (step ⑦)

Top-k ranking scores each unit alone, so a set of eight near-identical speech segments scores as well as a set that also contains the slide they refer to. The proposed reranker scores the set. Given candidates C with relevance rel(u), modality m(u), link edges E and embeddings eu, it selects R ⊆ C with |R| = k to maximise

F(R) = Σu∈R rel(u) + α · |{m(u) : u ∈ R}| + β · |{(u, v) ∈ R² : u→v ∈ E}| − γ · Σu≠v∈R, m(u)=m(v) cos(eu, ev).(7)

The three structural terms reward covering more modalities, prefer evidence linked to what is already selected, and charge for restating something already in the set. Redundancy is charged only within a modality, because a speech segment and the slide it describes are meant to be similar. Exact maximisation is a facility-location-style problem and is NP-hard, so selection is greedy: it starts from the most relevant unit and repeatedly adds the unit with the largest marginal gain

Δ(u | R) = rel(u) + α · [m(u) ∉ m(R)] + β · |{v ∈ R : u↔v ∈ E}| − γ · Σv∈R, m(v)=m(u) cos(eu, ev),(8)

with α = 0.3, β = 0.2 and γ = 0.4. Relevance must be on the same scale as these constants; Section 5.3 shows what happens otherwise. Two ablations share the same code path: plain top-k, and maximal marginal relevance [17], which is blind to modality and links. Figure 6 contrasts the two kinds of selection on one pool.

3.2.5 Answering, verification and re-query (steps ⑧–⑨)

The answerer receives the selected units with their identifiers and returns the answer as a list of claims, each citing the units it rests on. A separate judge model checks every claim against the text of the units it cites and must quote a supporting span; a claim without one is counted as unsupported. In the iterative mode, the answerer may issue one follow-up query after reading the first evidence set, following [4], and the union of both retrievals goes to the reranker.

3.3 Implementation details

LinkRAG is written in Python 3.11. BGE-M3 runs through sentence-transformers on PyTorch; dense search is an exact inner product in NumPy and sparse search uses rank-bm25. PDFs are parsed with PyMuPDF, OCR is Tesseract, speech recognition is faster-whisper, and the link graph is held in NetworkX. Every stage runs on a CPU (Apple M2, 8 GB). On LectQA-Vid, gpt-5.4-mini answers and gpt-4.1-mini judges, through the OpenAI API at temperature 0; every priced call goes to a cost ledger; the XFile agent sessions are not priced. On XFile, Claude Haiku 5.5 answers and Claude Sonnet 5.5 judges, run as agent sessions whose replies are cached; before use, the Sonnet judge was compared with the stored gpt-4.1-mini verdicts of an earlier pilot and agreed with Cohen's κ = 0.81 over three runs. On both benchmarks every reply is cached by its full request, so a re-run replays the stored answers exactly. An automatic check reads the session transcripts to verify that each answering session read its whole context before replying; over a third of the first gold-check answers failed it and were redone, and the check was run on every later answering round before its replies were used. BLEU-4 and METEOR use NLTK, and the MaViLS F1 uses the authors' scorer.

3.4 Experimental protocol

Compared systems. On XFile, the headline comparison is LinkRAG with link expansion and complementarity reranking against a no-links control: the same retriever and the same reranker with the link graph removed. This comparison was recorded in the repository (PLAN.md, with commit and time) before the test run; it was not externally registered, and it is called pre-registered below. The tune check (§4.1) showed that this control is unstable, most likely because the plain retriever passes raw fusion scores to the reranker (§5.3). LinkRAG is therefore also compared with plain top-k retrieval, which uses neither links nor the reranker; this second comparison was added after the tune check and before any test result existed. Figure 7 lays out the four XFile conditions and what each comparison isolates.

On LectQA-Vid, the baseline is a re-implementation of [2], whose code is not public, built with the values its Table 3 gives: semantic chunking, BGE-base embeddings, top-10 candidates above cosine 0.6, the temporal filter with a 3 s tolerance, merging of adjacent results, cross-encoder re-ranking and a temporally ordered context. Where [2] is silent, the choices are this work's: the re-rank pool and context sizes, the merge gap, the cross-encoder, and OCR in place of generated frame captions. Two of these choices matter for the results. The paper does not say where the filter's query interval comes from at inference, so the question's annotated interval is used, the only interval the paper describes. It does not say what happens when no chunk survives, so the re-implementation returns a fixed "no evidence" reply. Every LectQA-Vid method is answered by the same model and prompt. Because the answering model of [2] is not named, absolute scores cannot be compared with its tables, but differences between the rows reported here can. Reference rows need no retrieval: the full transcript given to the same answerer, the question returned as its own answer, and for multiple choice the share of correct options that are first or longest. On MaViLS, the baselines are the per-lecture results published in [3].

Constants. Every constant in Eqs. 1–8 (weights, penalties, thresholds, δ, α, β, γ) is a configuration default fixed during earlier development, before XFile was built, and none was changed for XFile; the relatedness threshold's calibration is given in §3.2.2.

Metrics. On XFile, an answer is correct when the judge, in a majority of three grading runs, finds that it states every fact of the reference answer; evidence recall@k is the share of a question's gold units that are among the k units passed to the answerer. LectQA-Vid uses the metric suite of [2]: token F1, BLEU-4 [18], METEOR [19], ROUGE-1 [20] and MiniLM cosine similarity for open-ended answers, and accuracy for multiple choice. MaViLS uses the authors' F1 per lecture over labelled sentences, averaged over lectures. Faithfulness is the share of claims the judge finds supported by the units they cite.

Statistics. Intervals are 95 % bootstrap intervals [21] from 10,000 resamples. Paired differences resample the sampled unit: topics on XFile, videos on LectQA-Vid and lectures on MaViLS, because questions on one topic or video share their sources. A difference whose interval contains zero is reported as no measurable difference. The XFile test half has only three topics, so a bootstrap over topics has ten distinct resamples and its 95 % interval reduces to the range of the per-topic differences: it excludes zero only when all three topics agree in sign, which a sign test would not call significant (p = 0.25). XFile differences are therefore given with their per-topic values, and no XFile difference is claimed as established in either direction. XFile results are means over three answering repeats.

4. Results

4.1 Cross-file question answering on XFile

Tune check. The three tune topics (36 questions) were run with the frozen defaults as a check, and no setting was changed afterwards. The check exposed one problem: the default reranker roughly halved the no-links control, from 63.9 % correct with plain top-k to 33.3 % with complementarity reranking, while LinkRAG reached 65.7 %. This is consistent with the score-scale issue of §5.3: in a later retrieval-only check, used for no setting, shrinking α, β and γ a hundredfold brought the control's recall back to the level of plain top-k. Because a comparison against a weakened control would overstate any gain, the test half is also reported against plain top-k.

Retrieval and coverage. The links change what is retrieved, but mostly in kind rather than in amount. An evidence set covers about one more modality than plain top-k's. Evidence recall is higher than the no-links control's, but only because the reranker had pushed the control's recall below plain top-k; against plain top-k the gain is about one point, comes from a single topic (gp_scaling), and is slightly negative on the tune topics. None of this turns into more correct answers. With re-querying, links again did not help: re-querying with links answered fewer cross-file questions than re-querying without them, and the best configuration overall was one re-query without links (61.4 %, a point estimate with no interval). The rejected Lightning topic was not evaluated.

The mechanism on one question. The question of §1.1 and Figure 1 (a tune question) shows what the layer is designed to do. Plain retrieval finds the spoken odds and the slide's text block, but not the figure on the same slide, which carries the study's name, so the answer is incomplete in all three runs, with or without the reranker. The same-slide link from the slide text brings the figure into LinkRAG's candidate pool, the reranker keeps it, and LinkRAG answers correctly in all three runs. It is one case, not a trend: on the tune topics LinkRAG beat plain top-k on two questions (this one and PS02) and lost on two (PS01 and PS04), one iterative re-query without links also answered this question in two of three runs, and on the test topics LinkRAG and plain top-k gave the same result on every question.

Only two steps are measurable (Table 5). The re-query, a technique from [4], accounts for most of the advantage; without it the two retrieval pipelines are level. The other measurable step is not a property of retrieval quality: it is the cost of answering "no evidence" when the temporal filter removes every candidate. In [2], removing the filter lowers F1 from 23.52 to 11.40; this is not reproduced. The filter left 34 questions with no context at all, and in every one a chunk inside the window had existed and was removed earlier, by the similarity threshold or the top-10 cut. Where the filter kept something, it made no measurable difference (Table 5). A check of the 34 empty windows, with 16 passing intervals as a control, located each reference answer in the timestamped transcript without showing the annotated interval, and compared the two. For 27 of the 34 the interval misses the spoken answer or the answer is never said, against 4 of the 16 controls. Most empty windows are therefore annotation problems, not retrieval failures, and a filter driven by an annotated interval is only as good as the annotation.

4.2.3 What the benchmark can measure

Three observations bound the meaning of an open-ended score. First, the full transcript beats the best retrieval at every difficulty level (by 2.04 F1 overall, CI [+1.08, +3.06]). No transcript is longer than about 1,500 tokens, and all 95 together are about 73,000, so a current model can read a whole video, or the whole corpus, at once; retrieval can only remove information. Second, reference answers restate the question, which is why echoing the question scores so well. Third, little more than half of the annotated intervals pass basic format and range checks. A closed-book control points the same way: a second model (Claude Haiku 5.5, one run) reaches three-quarters of its with-transcript score with no context at all.

4.2.4 Localisation and a pooled-corpus stress test

Without an answerer, retrieval was scored by whether a retrieved unit overlaps the annotated interval, on the questions with a checkable interval. Plain hybrid top-k finds the interval within its top eight for most questions. Link-following with complementarity reranking lowers hit@3 by 5.01 points [−7.60, −2.24] and makes no measurable difference at hit@8. LectQA-Vid has no separate deck, so a deck was recovered from the video frames and aligned with the speech; the alignment picks the slide on screen for under a quarter of speech segments, because spoken sentences and the OCR text of animated frames share little vocabulary. When all 95 videos are pooled into one index and each question is asked without its video, hit@8 falls from about 85 % to 53 %. Plain top-k remains the best method: the full linked system is about 7 points lower, and the links alone make no measurable difference. Most questions refer to "the video" or "the speaker", so pooling does not turn LectQA-Vid into a multi-source benchmark.

4.3 Speech-to-slide alignment on MaViLS

The audio–slide links of §3.2.2 depend on aligning speech to slides, and MaViLS measures exactly that. On this benchmark, the alignment is extended with two channels computed from the video frame at each sentence's timestamp: SwiftFormer image similarity [16] between frame and slide, and BM25 between the frame's OCR text and the slide's text. The three matrices are fused with weights set on the tune half and decoded by the dynamic programme of §3.2.2. A visibility gate neutralises the two frame channels when a frame shows no slide. The links on XFile are built by the text-only alignment of Eq. 1, which is the speech-only row of Table 7 (0.483 against the published audio-only 0.47); the frame channels need video and are used only here.

What produces the result. The frames do. Frame OCR alone comes close to the fused result, and removing the speech channel costs little: not measurably on the nine test lectures, and a small measurable amount over all 19; this agrees with [3], where OCR is the strongest single feature and audio the weakest. The decoder does not set the systems apart: on the fused matrix, the decoder of [3], run from the authors' code, and the proposed decoder are level over 19 lectures, and a jump-penalised decoder of either kind adds about 0.06 over no decoder. The benchmark has no trivial floor: mapping sentence position linearly onto the deck scores 0.21. On the tune half, the four weakest lectures are camera-heavy: many of their errors fall on frames that show no slide, and most lie within two slides of the correct one.

5. Discussion

5.1 When does linking help?

On LectQA-Vid, neither link-following nor set reranking improved retrieval. This is expected there: a LectQA-Vid question concerns one short video with no separate deck or document, so there are no files to link, and the temporal links that remain add nothing that hybrid retrieval does not already find. MaViLS evaluates alignment, not question answering; there the alignment matches the published aligner once video-frame channels are added, and its text-only form matches the published audio-only result. XFile is the first test of the setting the layer was designed for, and at its scale the result is null: links did not help answers: LinkRAG matched plain retrieval on every test question and fell below the no-links control on two of three topics, though three topics cannot establish that difference. Three readings fit the data. First, the test is small. With 21 cross-file questions on three topics, the bootstrap has only ten distinct resamples, so a modest effect could be hidden. Second, the links changed retrieval mainly by adding modalities; against plain top-k their recall gain is small and confined to one topic, so few of the units they added were the gold units the answers needed. Third, the layer was measured with its gate errors present (§5.4), which put links between unrelated lectures into a pooled index. On LectQA-Vid the one measurable gain came from re-querying, and on XFile the highest point estimate also came from re-querying without links. The result does not show that evidence linking cannot help. It shows that, as configured here, it did not improve answers on the first benchmark built to test it.

5.2 What the existing benchmarks can measure

The audit of §4.2.3 shows that LectQA-Vid cannot rank retrieval methods: its transcripts fit in one context window, its reference answers restate their questions, and its multiple-choice answers are recoverable from option length. MaViLS is sound, but it rewards frame-to-slide matching and leaves speaker shots unsolved. Neither contains a question whose evidence spans files, which is the reason XFile was built, with a per-source verification step that removes questions answerable from a single file.

5.3 A score-scale requirement for set reranking

The complementarity objective (Eq. 7) adds fixed bonuses (α = 0.3 per new modality) and penalties (γ = 0.4 times redundancy) to relevance scores. Reciprocal rank fusion yields scores near 0.03, so on raw scores the bonuses decide the selection almost alone. LectQA-Vid rows measured this way were withdrawn, and no reranker-only replacement was measured; the valid row is links plus reranker on rank-normalised scores, which lowers hit@3 by about 5 points against plain top-k (§4.2.4). Set-level objectives that mix relevance with structural terms should normalise relevance first, and ablations should check that all compared conditions see scores on one scale. In the proposed system the linked retriever passes rank-normalised relevance to the reranker, but the plain retriever passes raw fusion scores, so every "plain retriever + complementarity" row, including the no-links control on XFile, is affected. The effect is not even stable in sign: it roughly halved the control's correctness on the XFile tune topics and raised it slightly on the test topics (§4.1). Plain top-k, which involves no reranker, is the cleaner comparison.

5.4 Linking errors in a pooled corpus

The relatedness gate was calibrated on pairs of single lectures. In the pooled XFile corpus it admitted four pairs of unrelated lectures and rejected two recordings' own decks (Fig. 5), and almost half of the audio–slide links connect different lectures. A few figure–text links also join different topics, and every deictic link outside the rejected Lightning topic rides on a false recording–deck pair, which is why the deictic example in Fig. 4 comes from Lightning. A rule that links each recording to at most one deck, the one with the highest z, would remove all four false pairs on XFile, but it was not applied, because it was not fixed before the evaluation. The XFile results therefore measure the layer with these errors present.

6. Limitations

  • Size of XFile. The test half has three topics with 21 cross-file and 17 single-source questions. A bootstrap over three topics has only ten distinct resamples, so intervals are coarse, and a small effect may be reported as no measurable difference. The rejected Lightning topic was not evaluated.
  • How the XFile questions were made. Questions were drafted by a language model and reviewed by people, not written by them; the review first adopted a model pre-screen's decisions and the later reading changed none. The reviewers and auditors are authors of this paper, and the answerer belongs to the same model family as the drafter.
  • No XFile reference rows. A whole topic fits in a current model's context and the notes are well-known textbooks, yet XFile reports neither the full-context nor the closed-book row that §4.2.3 asks of LectQA-Vid. The gold check's all-source answers cannot stand in for a full-context row, because questions were kept only if that answer passed.
  • XFile models. Answers and judgements on XFile come from one model family (Claude Haiku answers, Claude Sonnet grades), run as agent sessions rather than API calls; they are cached and can be re-scored, but not re-generated identically, and the results are best read as direction only. Grading ran in batches of 25, so one grading session could see several answers to the same question.
  • Figure OCR. Figures are represented by their OCR text, which can miss small labels such as axis values that are visible on the slide image (§3.1.1).
  • Linking errors. The relatedness gate makes six errors on the pooled XFile corpus (§5.4).
  • Re-implementation of [2]. Several parameters and the source of the filter interval are this work's choices; with the authors' code or evaluation subset the comparison could differ. Open-ended results cover 300 of the 1,418 open-ended questions, with one answering model, and the closed-book control used a different model in a single run.
  • Annotation check. The LectQA-Vid timestamp check was done by a language model locating answers in the transcript, not by a person, and covers 75 intervals.
  • MaViLS. Test results rest on nine lectures, compared with published per-lecture figures rather than a re-run, and the test half was examined in several rounds, although no parameter was set on it.
  • Faithfulness. Claims were judged once per claim by a single model.

7. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper proposed LinkRAG, a lecture RAG system that links speech, slides, figures and documents at indexing time, follows those links at retrieval time, and selects evidence as a set, together with XFile, a benchmark whose questions are verified to need more than one file. LinkRAG's retrieval exceeds a controlled re-implementation of Temporal-Aware RAG by two F1 points, most of it from one re-query, and its alignment, extended with video-frame channels, matches MaViLS. On XFile, the first benchmark that can test cross-file linking, the links widened evidence sets across modalities but did not improve answers on the held-out topics, and three topics are too few to establish a difference either way. Neither published benchmark can show the effect of linking, because neither has evidence in more than one file, and the audit shows that LectQA-Vid cannot rank retrieval methods.

Future work.

  1. A larger cross-file benchmark. More topics and courses, questions written by people, and corpora whose total size exceeds one context window, so that retrieval cannot be bypassed. A null result on three test topics leaves room for an effect too small to detect here.
  2. One score scale for every reranked row. Rank-normalising relevance in the plain retriever as well would make the no-links control stable (§5.3).
  3. One deck per recording. Restricting each recording to its best-matching deck would remove the false pairs of §5.4; it should be fixed on tune topics and tested once on held-out topics.
  4. Speaker shots in slide alignment. On the weakest MaViLS lectures most errors fall on frames that show the speaker, where only the speech channel and the decoder decide.
  5. A reproduction with the original code of [2], if its code or evaluation subset is released.
  6. Human faithfulness judgements on a sample of claims, to bound the judge's errors.

Code and Data Availability

The code, the XFile questions with their verified evidence, the link graph, the per-question results and the stored model replies are available at github.com/m1ssmee/LinkRAG. Lecture transcripts and slides come from the MaViLS release [3] at a pinned commit; the notes chapters are third-party documents and are not redistributed, but each is identified by its source address, page range and SHA-256 hash, so the corpus can be rebuilt with the same evidence units.
